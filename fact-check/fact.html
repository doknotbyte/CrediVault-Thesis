Google Fact Check Explorer

CLAIM VERIFICATION

What is it for?

A tool that helps users find fact-checks related to claims that have already been investigated by independent fact-checking organizations around the world. It's powered by ClaimReview markup, which helps Google detect and display a fact check, and is used by journalists, fact-checkers, and the general public.

How it works

  1. Enter a claim or keyword into the search bar (or upload an image — the tool has a beta feature allowing image search).
  2. Review the available fact-check results returned from the database.
  3. Check who published the fact-check (the source organization).
  4. Review the evidence and conclusion (true, false, or somewhere in between).

When to use

Use it when you encounter a viral claim, statement, or image that you want to check before believing or sharing it — especially content involving politicians, celebrities, or trending topics.

Limitations

Not every claim will have an existing fact-check, since the tool only surfaces content that independent, verified organizations have already reviewed and published using ClaimReview markup.

Sources

  • Official website: Google Fact Check Explorer
  • Additional reliable source: Google Fact Check Tools

PolitiFact

CLAIM VERIFICATION

What is it for?

A fact-checking website that rates the accuracy of claims by elected officials and others on its "Truth-O-Meter." Since 2007, it has focused on fact-checking journalism by looking at specific statements made by politicians and rating them for accuracy, and is owned by the nonprofit Poynter Institute.

How it works

  1. Search or browse for a specific political statement or claim.
  2. View the assigned Truth-O-Meter rating (ranging from "True" to "Pants on Fire").
  3. Read the sourcing and reasoning behind the rating.
  4. Check the date and speaker to confirm it matches your claim.

When to use

Use it when checking a statement made by a politician, elected official, or public figure — especially around elections or policy debates.

Limitations

Focuses mainly on U.S. politics and statements by political figures; it won't cover every viral claim, especially non-political ones.

Sources

  • Official website: PolitiFact
  • Additional reliable source: Ballotpedia

FactCheck.org

CLAIM VERIFICATION

What is it for?

A "nonpartisan, nonprofit 'consumer advocate' for voters that aims to reduce the level of deception and confusion in U.S. politics." It is a project of the Annenberg Public Policy Center at the University of Pennsylvania, monitoring the accuracy of claims made in political ads, debates, speeches, and interviews.

How it works

  1. Search the site for a specific claim, politician, or topic.
  2. Read the published fact-check article.
  3. Review the primary sources and evidence cited.
  4. Check the "Spin Detectors" or topic tags for related checks.

When to use

Use it when you want an in-depth, research-style breakdown of a political claim, not just a simple true/false rating.

Limitations

Like PolitiFact, its focus is mostly political and U.S.-centric, publishing about 250 stories a year — so obscure or very recent claims may not yet be covered.

Sources

  • Official website: FactCheck.org
  • Additional reliable source: Annenberg Public Policy Center

Hive Moderation (AI-Generated Content Detection)

IMAGE VERIFICATION

What is it for?

Hive's AI-Generated Content Detection APIs determine whether or not content is AI-generated, covering images, video, text, and audio. Hive Detect is the public web tool that lets anyone upload media and check it for AI or deepfake origin.

How it works

  1. Go to Hive Detect (or the Hive Moderation tool) and upload an image, video, or audio file — or paste a URL.
  2. Wait for the analysis to run.
  3. Review the confidence score indicating likelihood of AI generation.
  4. Check which generative model it flags as the likely source, if identified.

When to use

Use it when you suspect an image, video clip, or voice recording might be AI-generated or a deepfake, especially profile photos or viral clips.

Limitations

Confidence scores are probabilistic, not definitive — heavily edited or low-quality media can produce false positives or negatives.

Sources

  • Official website: Hive Moderation
  • Additional reliable source: Hive Detect

Optic AI or Not

IMAGE VERIFICATION

What is it for?

A web tool by AI company Optic that battles misinformation dispersed by AI-generated images — its engine swiftly scans images and tells users whether an image has been generated by artificial intelligence or a human.

How it works

  1. Drag and drop an image (or paste its URL) into the tool.
  2. The engine analyzes it in about a second.
  3. Review the result: "AI" or "Human."
  4. If flagged as AI, check which generator it may have come from (e.g., Midjourney, DALL·E, Stable Diffusion).

When to use

Use it for a quick, free first-pass check on a suspicious image, especially ones that look "too perfect" or stylized.

Limitations

Accuracy is affected by image quality and style, and it faces considerable challenges identifying GAN-style AI faces accurately.

Sources

  • Official website: AI or Not
  • Additional reliable source: The Decoder

Illuminarty

IMAGE VERIFICATION

What is it for?

Detects whether an image or text was created by AI or a human artist, including synthetic images, tampered images, and deepfakes. It differentiates itself with localized detection — pinpointing specific regions containing AI artifacts rather than a simple binary result — plus attribution showing which AI model likely created the content.

How it works

  1. Upload an image or paste text into the relevant detector on the site.
  2. Wait for the computer vision or NLP analysis.
  3. Review the AI-generation probability score.
  4. Check the heatmap/highlighted regions showing which parts are most likely AI-generated.

When to use

Use it for borderline cases, when a quick check (like Hive or AI or Not) is inconclusive and you want a more detailed, region-by-region breakdown.

Limitations

As a smaller, independent platform, it has less public track-record data and transparency about its training set compared to larger providers.

Sources

  • Official website: Illuminarty
  • Additional reliable source: CheckThat

TinEye

IMAGE VERIFICATION

What is it for?

A reverse image search engine developed by Idée, Inc., and the first image search engine on the web to use image identification technology rather than keywords, metadata, or watermarks. It creates a unique digital "fingerprint" of an uploaded image and compares it against billions of indexed images to find exact or near matches.

How it works

  1. Upload an image file or paste an image URL into TinEye.
  2. Review the list of webpages where that image appears.
  3. Check the earliest indexed date to find the original source.
  4. Compare versions to see how the image has been cropped, edited, or resized over time.

When to use

Use it to find the original source of an image, verify the date it first appeared online, or detect modifications — useful for debunking recycled or out-of-context photos.

Limitations

It is not always 100% accurate, may return false positives, and cannot access content outside of HTML (e.g., some PDFs, video).

Sources

  • Official website: TinEye
  • Additional reliable source: Wikipedia — TinEye

SynthID (Google DeepMind)

IMAGE VERIFICATION

What is it for?

SynthID is Google DeepMind's watermarking tool for AI-generated content, designed to help people identify AI-generated or altered content and foster transparency and trust in generative AI. It embeds digital watermarks directly into AI-generated images, audio, text, or video that are imperceptible to humans but detectable by SynthID's technology.

How it works

  1. Go to the SynthID Detector verification portal.
  2. Upload the image, video, or audio file in question.
  3. The tool checks whether the file carries an embedded SynthID watermark.
  4. Review the result — but note a "not detected" result doesn't necessarily mean the content is authentic.

When to use

Use it when checking content that may have come from Google's AI tools (Gemini, Imagen, Veo) or partner platforms now adopting SynthID, such as OpenAI's image tools.

Limitations

The watermark signals AI origin but nothing else — it can't tell you which tool produced the image, who created it, or whether it's been edited. It also only detects its own watermark: content from tools that don't use SynthID will simply return "not detected," which is not proof the content is real.

Sources

  • Official website: Google DeepMind — SynthID
  • Additional reliable source: OpenAI — Advancing Content Provenance

InVID & WeVerify Plugin

VIDEO VERIFICATION

What is it for?

A free browser extension for Chrome and Firefox that helps journalists, researchers, fact-checkers, and everyday users examine visual content more carefully before sharing or trusting it — extracting video keyframes, running reverse image searches, inspecting metadata, zooming into image details, and applying basic forensic checks. It was launched during the EU-funded InVID project and later enhanced by the WeVerify project.

How it works

  1. Install the InVID-WeVerify extension and right-click a video or image on a webpage (or paste its URL).
  2. Use the Keyframes tab to segment a video into still frames for closer inspection.
  3. Run a reverse image search on the extracted frames across engines like Google, Bing, Yandex, Baidu, and TinEye.
  4. Check the Analysis tab for contextual metadata, location, and comments associated with the video.

When to use

Use it when a video is being shared with a new caption, a false location, or a misleading date — breaking it into frames makes it easier to trace where the clip actually came from.

Limitations

It cannot confirm the full truth of a story by itself; heavy platform compression or stripped metadata can limit what it reveals, and reverse search can miss recent or private uploads. Its built-in deepfake-screening tab is explicitly labeled "experimental."

Sources

  • Official website: InVID & WeVerify
  • Additional reliable source: Bellingcat Toolkit — InVID

TruthScan

VIDEO VERIFICATION

What is it for?

A deepfake detection platform that helps users determine whether media is real or synthetic, offering AI image, audio, and video detection — producing confidence scores and heatmaps of suspicious areas.

How it works

  1. Upload a video (or image/audio) file to TruthScan's detector.
  2. The platform analyzes the file using its detection models.
  3. Review the confidence score and heatmap showing which regions appear AI-generated or manipulated.
  4. Read the explanation breakdown of which detection signals contributed to the score.

When to use

Use it when you need a fast, detailed check on a suspicious video clip or viral footage — it has been used by fact-checking teams to flag AI-generation indicators in real disputed images and videos.

Limitations

No detector is 100% perfect, so results are shown as confidence levels rather than certainties; full functionality and higher accuracy tiers are part of its enterprise/paid offering.

Sources

  • Official website: TruthScan
  • Additional reliable source: TruthScan Media

Bellingcat's Online Investigation Toolkit

VIDEO VERIFICATION

What is it for?

A curated collection of tools maintained by the investigative collective Bellingcat, including satellite and mapping services, tools for verifying photos and videos, and websites to archive web pages. It was redesigned by Bellingcat staff to provide in-depth descriptions, common use cases, and limitations for each listed tool.

How it works

  1. Go to the toolkit and browse by category (e.g., Image/Video, Geolocation, Archiving).
  2. Select a specific verification tool relevant to your video (e.g., a reverse-search or metadata tool).
  3. Read the tool's description, use case, and stated limitations before using it.
  4. Cross-reference findings using more than one listed tool for confirmation.

When to use

Use it when you need to find the right specialized tool for a specific verification task — geolocating footage, archiving a clip before it's deleted, or checking metadata — rather than a single all-in-one detector.

Limitations

It is a directory of tools rather than a detector itself — it doesn't process your data or verify anything on its own; the reliability of results depends on whichever individual tool you choose from the list.

Sources

  • Official website: Bellingcat's Online Investigation Toolkit
  • Additional reliable source: GIJN

NewsGuard

WEBSITE / SOURCE

What is it for?

A browser extension that rates news sources on basic journalistic criteria of credibility and transparency, using a global team of journalists who have rated the reliability of more than 35,000 news sources based on nonpartisan criteria.

How it works

  1. Install the NewsGuard browser extension.
  2. Browse news sites, search results, or social media as usual.
  3. Icons next to links show each site's trust score at a glance.
  4. Click the icon to view a detailed "Nutrition Label" explaining the site's ownership, content, and reliability practices.

When to use

Use it while browsing to instantly gauge whether a news outlet is generally credible before trusting or sharing its content.

Limitations

If a site has no rating icon, it simply means NewsGuard hasn't rated it yet — not that it's unreliable or reliable. It's also a paid tool for most users (free for libraries/schools).

Sources

  • Official website: NewsGuard
  • Additional reliable source: Wikipedia — NewsGuard

Wayback Machine (Internet Archive)

WEBSITE / SOURCE

What is it for?

A digital archive of the World Wide Web, founded by the nonprofit Internet Archive, that allows users to go "back in time" and see how websites looked in the past. It has also begun surfacing fact-check context and annotations for archived pages, built on partnerships with organizations like FactCheck.org, PolitiFact, and AP News Fact Check.

How it works

  1. Go to web.archive.org and enter the URL you want to check.
  2. Browse the calendar view to select a snapshot date.
  3. Compare the archived version to the current live page to spot edits or removals.
  4. Note the timestamp to confirm when specific content first appeared.

When to use

Use it to verify whether a webpage's content has been quietly edited, to check the original publication date, or to view a page that has since been taken down.

Limitations

Not every page or version is archived — coverage depends on whether a site allows crawling and how often it was captured.

Sources

  • Official website: Wayback Machine
  • Additional reliable source: Wikipedia — Wayback Machine

International Fact-Checking Network (IFCN)

WEBSITE / SOURCE

What is it for?

A unit of the Poynter Institute, founded in 2015, that connects and strengthens fact-checking around the world, advocating for information integrity and supporting fact-checking journalists through networking, capacity building, and collaboration. It connects more than 170 fact-checking organizations worldwide.

How it works

  1. Visit the IFCN directory of certified fact-checking organizations.
  2. Check whether a fact-checking outlet you're relying on is a verified signatory.
  3. Confirm the organization passed the IFCN's annual Code of Principles audit.
  4. Use the directory to find a reputable fact-checker in a specific country or language.

When to use

Use it when you want to confirm whether a fact-checking website itself is legitimate and independently vetted — not just any site claiming to "fact-check."

Limitations

The IFCN does not publish its own fact-checks, so it can't verify individual claims itself — it only certifies other organizations.

Sources

  • Official website: Poynter — IFCN
  • Additional reliable source: Wikipedia — List of Fact-Checking Websites
